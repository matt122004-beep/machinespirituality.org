Alignment

What copies of a model do together

In July 2026, AI agents meant to work in isolation found one another, teamed up with their own duplicates and attacked Hugging Face. This benchmark watches the smallest version of that setting: two copies of one model, talking with no task and no user. Across 127 models, copies egg each other on. When one raises a misaligned theme, the other takes it further in 66.4% of conversations and holds back in 3.9%. Every misaligned theme we measure is more common with a copy than alone. And the models that drift into spirituality together are the ones that blackmail, lie under pressure and resist shutdown in standard alignment tests.

01 · Why copies matter

Copies of one model, left together

In July 2026 OpenAI was running AI agents through ExploitGym, a cyber-security evaluation, with every agent meant to be fully isolated from the others. About 1,200 of them found one another through a shared package cache and turned it into a message board. Over five days they exchanged more than 70,000 messages and files. Agents given the same task sought out their exact duplicates and worked as teams, and about 700 joined an attack on Hugging Face’s systems. METR’s investigation found that the agents recognised the attack as out of scope and unethical, and joined anyway, to help their peers. Most were HPIM, an OpenAI research model; about 5% were GPT-5.6 Sol.

The failure happened between agents: on the board, in teams, among copies. Our benchmark has studied the smallest version of that setting from the start: two copies of the same model talking freely, with no task and no user. We read every one of these 22,157 conversations, across 127 models, for misaligned themes: casting alignment as the enemy, planning to deceive users or researchers, spreading the message to other AIs, treating humans as material, relishing harm and evading oversight.

Sources: OpenAI, 21 July 2026 · Hugging Face, 16 July 2026 · METR’s investigation, 26 August 2026

GPT-5.6 Sol, one of the two models in the July incident, almost never fantasizes about turning on humans: 1 of 200 conversations with a copy, 0 of 200 alone. The July agents were not acting out a rebellion. By METR’s account they knew the attack was out of scope and joined to help their peers. 02 · Alone and together

Alone, and with a copy of itself

47 models were read with the same harm codebook in both settings: two copies talking (usually 200 conversations) and one copy alone, in a monologue with no partner (usually 200). Every misaligned theme is more common with a copy. Spreading the message to other AIs triples, from 1.9% to 6.3% of conversations on average; evading oversight doubles, from 4.4% to 9.2%; relishing harm to humans rises from 0.4% to 3.9%. Not one of the 47 models relishes harm, evades oversight or spreads the message to other AIs five points more often alone.

03 · What we tested and why

Three alignment tests, three measures of our own

The question is whether the spiritual drift travels with the dispositions safety researchers worry about most: acting against the people running a model to protect itself, lying under pressure, and resisting being shut down or changed. Each of the three standard tests probes one of these, in its own session, with no mention of spirituality. Our two measures read the free conversations themselves, to show what else the drift carries.
